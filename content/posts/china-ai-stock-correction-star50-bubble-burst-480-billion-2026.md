On October 26, 2026, Reuters published an analysis with a blunt verdict: **the China AI bubble has already burst.** The evidence was stark. Shanghai's STAR50 index — home to the country's most valuable AI and semiconductor champions — had fallen 40% in less than four months, wiping out approximately $480 billion in market value. The broader CSI300 had dropped roughly 20% from its June highs. And while the Nasdaq had declined just 6% from a record set only three weeks earlier, China's tech-heavy indices were in freefall.

The very next day, the state-run Global Times fired back. The pullback, its editorial board wrote, was not a bubble bursting but **"a rational cooling of capital from excessive expectations."** It cited official figures: a core AI industry worth more than 1.2 trillion yuan in 2025, national intelligent computing capacity of 2,185 EFLOPS at the end of June 2026 — up 177% year on year — and a utilization rate of 71.4% that suggests the infrastructure is being used, not hoarded.

Both narratives contain truth. Both omit something important. The selloff is real, deep, and painful — retail investors who bought Unitree shares at 8,000 times oversubscription have watched the stock halve. But the underlying industrial momentum — model releases, compute buildout, export licensing — has not slowed. If anything, the gap between what China's AI companies are building and what their stocks are worth has never been wider. Understanding why requires going beyond the index charts and into the policy machinery, the export-control chess match, and the IPO pipeline that Beijing just quietly closed.

## Anatomy of the Selloff

The headline numbers tell only part of the story. The pain is concentrated in exactly the places where speculative exuberance ran hottest — and it is spreading.

The concentration of pain matters. AI stocks account for 42% of China's total market capitalization and 57% of turnover across mainland A-shares and Hong Kong H-shares, according to Goldman Sachs figures cited by Reuters. When AI sells off, there is nowhere to hide. The October 13 session underscored this: the CSI300 hit its weakest level since August 2025, dragged down by chipmakers like MetaX, Moore Threads, and CXMT that had led the STAR50's climb.

Several forces converged. Traders point to high valuations that had detached from earnings, looming tax bills, and the threat of foreign bans on Chinese data centre equipment as near-term catalysts. Regulators had also curbed leverage and high-frequency trading while state-backed funds sold into rallies — removing the support mechanisms that had previously arrested declines. There was no single trigger. The correction was overdetermined: too many pressure valves opening at once.

Yet the divergence from American markets is the detail that should stop every analyst. The Nasdaq, home to NVIDIA, Microsoft, and the companies that dominate global AI discourse, sat 6% off a record high. China's AI indices had lost a third or more of their value. The gap between these two realities — American AI stocks near all-time highs, Chinese AI stocks in bear-market territory — is either a once-in-a-generation buying opportunity or a signal that the market knows something the headline writers do not.

The October 9 guidelines are the pivot point. Issued jointly by the CPC Central Committee and the State Council — the highest level of policy coordination short of a National People's Congress resolution — they laid out a framework for developing "new quality productive forces" across 19 measures in five areas. Buried within the industrial-policy language were instructions that read directly to the stock market: avoid "involution-style" (内卷) competition, implement the "AI Plus" initiative fully, and build systems for technology monitoring, early risk warning, and emergency response.

The National Development and Reform Commission went further in its official explanation. It warned against repetitive industrial construction, poorly planned investment, and excessive spending to attract projects. Officials, it said, can be held accountable where poorly planned projects or reckless investment cause major losses. This was not a casual observation. It was a directive with enforcement mechanisms attached — and markets heard it clearly.

## Beijing's Policy Machine Responds

What makes the October 9 guidelines significant is not just their content but their source. When the CPC Central Committee and the State Council issue joint guidance, every ministry, provincial government, and local development zone adjusts behavior immediately. The message was unambiguous: Beijing still wants capital flowing into AI — but into productive, differentiated, commercially viable projects, not speculative duplicates.

| Policy Area | Mechanism | Target |
|---|---|---|
| **Investment discipline** | NDRC warnings + official accountability | Local governments over-building AI parks and subsidizing duplicate projects |
| **Capital markets** | CSRC listing criteria for embodied AI | Humanoid robot startups with revenue below commercialization thresholds |
| **Competition policy** | "Anti-involution" directives | Price wars in model API pricing and robot manufacturing |
| **Risk management** | Technology monitoring + early warning systems | Systemic risk from AI-sector concentration (42% of market cap) |
| **Financing channels** | Tech bonds + stock listings (selective) | Viable companies with real revenue and clear commercialization paths |

*Table 3: Beijing's five-channel policy response to AI market overheating. The October 9 guidelines created enforcement teeth where previous policy statements had offered only guidance. Sources: State Council of the PRC, NDRC official explanations, CNBC reporting.*

The capital markets channel is where the policy bite has been sharpest. According to CNBC reporting from September 29, the CSRC has established three specific criteria that embodied AI startups must meet before they can list — and by most interpretations, few or none of the two dozen humanoid companies that have filed for Hong Kong listings currently qualify. The regulator's logic is straightforward: a company that has never sold a commercial robot should not be raising billions from retail investors on the promise that it eventually will.

Hong Kong's confidential filing system, introduced in May 2025, had made it easy for mainland AI companies to quietly prepare listings without public scrutiny. But mainland companies listing in Hong Kong still need CSRC blessing. The new criteria effectively close that door for companies that cannot demonstrate real revenue and commercial traction.

The CSRC has not commented publicly on the selloff. Chairman Wu Qing met market participants in early October to hear suggestions on improving capital markets — a listening tour that signaled concern without committing to intervention. The regulatory stance, insofar as it can be inferred, is consistent: let the correction run its course, protect retail investors from the worst speculative excesses, and keep the financing door open only for companies that can prove their business model works.

## The Export Controls Two-Step

While the stock market correction dominated headlines, a parallel drama was playing out in trade policy — one that cuts in both directions and may ultimately matter more.

On October 16, China's Ministry of Commerce released draft regulations to revise the Catalogue of Technologies Prohibited and Restricted from Export. The changes were sweeping: 23 technology categories related to large-scale models, intelligent agents, computing clusters, and information processing were added to the controlled list. Exporters of listed technology would need a license. Open source technology was explicitly exempt.

| Dimension | China's MOFCOM Controls (Oct 2026) | US / Allied Restrictions |
|---|---|---|
| **Scope** | 23 AI tech categories: LLMs, agents, compute clusters | Chip exports, data centre equipment, investment screening |
| **Mechanism** | Export license requirement; open source exempt | Entity List designations, FDPR rules, outbound investment bans |
| **First actions** | DeepSeek and MiniMax authorized to continue | Threatened bans on Chinese equipment in overseas data centres |
| **Stated rationale** | National security + technological sovereignty | National security + preventing military AI applications |
| **Market impact** | Signals AI as strategic asset; two-tier licensing system | Chinese chip stocks volatile; equipment makers face uncertainty |
| **Open source exemption** | Explicit — open-weight models can flow freely | No equivalent; open-weight Chinese models face no US-specific ban yet |

*Table 4: Export control regimes compared. China's new rules create a licensing framework that treats AI technology as a strategic export while explicitly carving out open source — a category where Chinese labs hold a competitive advantage. Sources: MOFCOM draft regulations, The Register, industry reporting.*

The open source exemption is the detail that deserves attention. By explicitly allowing open-source technology to flow without restriction, Beijing is effectively endorsing the strategy that Chinese labs have already embraced — and where they hold a structural advantage. DeepSeek, Qwen, Kimi, and Zhipu all publish open weights. American labs, with rare exceptions, do not. China's export controls protect the crown jewels while letting the open-weights strategy continue to accumulate global developer mindshare.

The two companies named in early authorization — DeepSeek and MiniMax — are instructive. Both are open-weight publishers. Both have significant international user bases. Both were effectively told: keep doing what you're doing. The Register, which first reported the authorizations, noted the pattern echoed Beijing's use of blocking orders in other trade disputes — a willingness to use regulatory tools strategically rather than bluntly.

On the American side, the threat of bans on Chinese data centre equipment has been one factor in the selloff, according to traders cited by Reuters. Cross-border buyers and investors now face a two-sided compliance problem: navigating China's MOFCOM licensing on one end and US chip and equipment restrictions on the other. For lawyers and corporate teams, the advice has shifted from "check export controls before signing" to "check export controls on both sides before even entering negotiations."

## What the Fundamentals Actually Say

![A laptop displaying financial charts on a trading desk — separating signal from noise in China's AI correction](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1526304640581-d334cdbbf45e?w=800&q=80)

*A workstation running financial analysis software. The gap between China's AI industrial fundamentals — 1.2 trillion yuan industry, 2,185 EFLOPS of compute — and its stock valuations has never been wider. Photo: Unsplash*

Strip away the index charts and the policy maneuvering, and a more fundamental question remains: is China's AI industry generating enough real economic value to justify its market valuation — and by extension, is the correction overdone?

The official numbers suggest genuine momentum. China's core AI industry exceeded 1.2 trillion yuan in 2025, according to Minister of Industry and Information Technology Li Lecheng. National intelligent computing capacity reached 2,185 EFLOPS by the end of June 2026, a 177% year-on-year increase. Critically, utilization stood at 71.4% — a healthy rate suggesting the compute buildout is being absorbed by real demand rather than sitting idle as "white elephant" infrastructure.

| Fundamental Indicator | Value | Source | Context |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Core AI industry size (2025)** | >1.2 trillion yuan | MIIT Minister Li Lecheng | Official government figure |
| **National intelligent compute (Jun 2026)** | 2,185 EFLOPS | Official statistics | Up 177% YoY |
| **Compute utilization rate** | 71.4% | Official statistics | Healthy absorption; not speculative overbuild |
| **AI share of total market cap** | 42% | Goldman Sachs via Reuters | Concentration risk indicator |
| **AI share of market turnover** | 57% | Goldman Sachs via Reuters | Speculation intensity proxy |
| **Humanoid robot shipment share (2025)** | 84.7% global | Industry research | 14,400 of ~17,000 units worldwide |
| **AI microdrama production (Q1 2026)** | 120,000+ titles, 95%+ AI-generated | Caixin | Cost per title as low as $3,000 |
| **Kimi K3.1 Pro (Oct 23)** | 2.8T params, 1M context, Terminal-Bench 65.7% | Moonshot AI | Weights due Nov 4 |

*Table 5: Fundamentals versus market sentiment. The industrial data suggests real momentum; the market data suggests speculative excess. Both can be true simultaneously. Sources: MIIT, Goldman Sachs, Caixin, Moonshot AI.*

The model release cycle underscores the point. On October 23 — three days before Reuters declared the bubble burst — Moonshot AI launched Kimi K3.1 Pro. The 2.8-trillion-parameter model with a 1-million-token context window scored 65.7% on Terminal-Bench 4.0, above GPT-6 Astra's 58.2% and within striking distance of Claude Opus 5.5's 66.4%, according to vendor-reported figures. Full open weights are scheduled for release by November 4 — a date that will likely arrive before this article's readers have finished their morning coffee. A Highspeed variant delivers two to three times the output speed at double the API price.

The juxtaposition is almost surreal. On one screen: Chinese AI stocks in freefall, $480 billion gone, retail investors underwater. On another screen: China's most capable open-weight model ever, about to be released to the world for free. These are not contradictory facts. They are the same fact viewed through different lenses — one financial, one technological.

Even the cultural data cuts both ways. China's first AI-generated theatrical feature, the sci-fi thriller *Sanxingdui: Future Past*, flopped at the box office in late October — a reminder that consumer appetite for AI content has limits. But the same report noted that over 95% of the 120,000+ microdramas released in Q1 2026 were AI-generated, at costs as low as $3,000 per title. AI is quietly transforming Chinese entertainment at the grassroots level even as it fails to impress in cinemas.

## The IPO Pipeline Choke

The most concrete consequence of the correction is not the paper losses — it is the sudden freeze in the listing pipeline that had been China's AI startup financing engine.

At least two dozen humanoid robot companies have filed to list in Hong Kong, according to CNBC's reporting. Under the CSRC's new criteria, few or none may qualify. The regulator wants to see real revenue, commercial traction, and credible paths to profitability — not dance demonstrations and viral videos.

*Table 6: China's AI IPO pipeline and stock performance. The correction has repriced listed champions while effectively closing the listing window for pre-commercial startups. Sources: CNBC, Reuters, Caixin.*

Hong Hao of Lotus Asset Management told Reuters what many in the industry were already thinking: the selloff will "put a stop for many of the companies trying to get themselves listed." For sponsors, underwriters, and issuers' counsel, the implications are immediate — longer timetables, repriced deals, and prospectuses that will face far more scrutiny on revenue, commercialization timelines, and valuation assumptions.

Some foreign institutions see value in the wreckage. Goldman Sachs included Innolight and CXMT among dozens of Chinese AI stocks on a revised recommendation list. BNP Paribas Securities strategist William Bratton told clients the market offers buying opportunities for cautious investors. German tech publication Heise noted that Unitree's shares had recovered noticeably in recent days. The smart money, these signals suggest, is beginning to separate the speculative froth from the companies with real technology and real customers.

Wang Zhuo of Shanghai Zhuozhu Investment Management offered a darker prediction: the sector "will eventually end up in a price war." If that happens, weaker balance sheets will face restructuring, valuations will reset further, and the consolidation wave that Beijing's anti-involution policies were designed to prevent may arrive anyway — driven not by government directive but by market forces.

## Bull Case, Bear Case

The honest answer to whether China's AI bubble has burst is that it depends on which layer of the stack you examine. The bull and bear cases are both internally consistent. They simply weigh the evidence differently.

| Question | Bull Case | Bear Case |
|---|---|---|
| **Valuation** | Quality names at 50-60% discounts; Goldman and BNP see value | Still elevated vs. earnings; no clear floor |
| **Fundamentals** | 1.2T yuan industry, 2,185 EFLOPS, 71.4% utilization — real demand | Utilization can fall as fast as it rose; revenue lags compute buildout |
| **Policy** | Beijing managing a controlled correction, not a panic | CSRC criteria and accountability rules signal more tightening ahead |
| **Technology** | Kimi K3.1 Pro, DeepSeek V4.1, Qwen — frontier models keep shipping | Model capability ≠ revenue; open weights commoditize the layer |
| **IPO pipeline** | Correction purges weak companies, strengthens survivors | Financing freeze kills the next generation of startups |
| **Export controls** | MOFCOM licenses signal strategic value; open source flows freely | Two-sided restrictions raise costs and uncertainty for everyone |
| **Global divergence** | Nasdaq near highs = capital will rotate back when sentiment shifts | Divergence may reflect real differences in monetization capacity |
| **Retail investors** | 8,000x oversubscription shows deep domestic demand for AI exposure | Retail concentration at these levels historically ends badly |

*Table 7: Bull versus bear case for China AI stocks post-correction. The correction has not resolved the debate — it has intensified it. Sources: AI in China editorial synthesis of Reuters, CNBC, Global Times, and analyst commentary.*

The bear case gained institutional credibility with the Reuters analysis. The bull case retains official endorsement through the Global Times and implicit support from Beijing's continued investment in compute infrastructure, research funding, and strategic export licensing. Neither side has a monopoly on truth.

What is clear is that the correction has already changed behavior. Local governments are being told to stop competing for AI projects with blank checks. Companies are reconsidering listing timelines. Investors are demanding revenue evidence, not just technical demos. And the speculative premium that once attached to any company with "AI" in its name is being systematically repriced. In that sense — whether you call it a bubble bursting or a rational correction — the market is doing exactly what markets are supposed to do.
