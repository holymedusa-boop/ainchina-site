On November 6, in a conference hall in Guangzhou, China Mobile chairman Chen Zhongyue delivered a presentation that would have been unrecognizable to a telecom executive from any previous era. There were no slides about subscriber growth, no ARPU charts, no 5G base station counts. Instead, Chen talked about gigawatts.

Three gigawatt-scale computing campuses had already broken ground in 2026, he said. Two more would start construction in 2027, bringing the total to seven. Several 10,000-GPU intelligent computing clusters were already operational. The company's intelligent computing capacity would double for two consecutive years. And the investment figure that stopped every analyst in the room: **more than 100 billion yuan — roughly $14 to $15 billion — dedicated to computing power in 2027 alone**, as part of over 500 billion yuan in total procurement planned for the year.

To put that in perspective, China Mobile's entire capital expenditure in 2024 was approximately 165 billion yuan. The company is now proposing to spend the equivalent of 60% of its total annual capex — on compute alone.

The world's largest wireless carrier, serving roughly one billion mobile subscribers, is no longer in the business of connecting phones to the internet. It is in the business of building the infrastructure that produces intelligence. And the comparison it invites — with American telecom giants on one side and US hyperscalers on the other — reveals just how radically the definition of a "telecom company" is being rewritten in China.

## The Announcement: Numbers Without Precedent

The November 6 presentation at the 2026 Mobile Network Conference was not a single announcement but a cascade of them. Chen Zhongyue, who took over as chairman with a mandate to accelerate China Mobile's AI transformation, laid out a procurement and investment plan that repositions the company from a connectivity provider to what Chinese industry officials increasingly call a "compute-network-energy" integrated operator.

The headline figure — over 100 billion yuan in compute investment for 2027 — comes embedded in a broader spending envelope that signals strategic intent more than incremental budgeting.

| China Mobile 2027 Investment Commitments | Amount (CNY) | Amount (USD) | Strategic Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Computing power investment | 100B+ yuan | $14–15B | AI training/inference clusters, GPU procurement |
| Terminal device procurement | 100B+ yuan | $14–15B | 100M+ units, AI-capable smartphones and devices |
| Channel resource deployment | 10B+ yuan | $1.4B+ | Distribution and sales network for AI services |
| Total procurement plan | 500B+ yuan | $70B+ | All categories combined for 2027 |
| Gigawatt-scale computing campuses | 7 total | — | 3 started 2026, 2 more in 2027 |
| GPU cluster deployment | Multiple 10,000-GPU clusters | — | Already operational as of November 2026 |

Sources: China Mobile 2026 Mobile Network Conference, Caixin Global, November 6, 2026.

The terminal procurement number deserves attention. China Mobile is not merely building the pipes and the compute — it is also ensuring that the devices running on its network are AI-capable, creating a full-stack ecosystem from silicon to handset to cloud. The company is targeting sales of over 100 million units, effectively guaranteeing a massive installed base for AI applications that will run on its infrastructure.

This is vertical integration at a scale that no private company — not even Alibaba or ByteDance — can match in China's market. China Mobile's status as a state-owned enterprise gives it access to procurement channels, regulatory advantages, and financing costs that private competitors cannot replicate.

## The Traffic Economics: Why a Telecom Became a Compute Company

The investment case for this pivot rests on data that Chen presented with precision. AI is not an abstract future opportunity for China Mobile — it is already transforming the traffic patterns on its network in ways that demand a fundamentally different infrastructure architecture.

The numbers tell a story of exponential change.

| AI Impact on China Mobile Network (2026) | Metric | Change | Implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mobile internet traffic growth | +18% YoY | 2x last year's growth rate | AI applications driving data consumption |
| AI-generated content traffic | 5x increase | Single-year multiple | Generated media now a major traffic source |
| Traditional traffic pattern (download:upload) | 9:1 ratio | Baseline | Consumer internet era pattern |
| AI-era traffic pattern | Shifting toward 1:1 | Inversion | Users generating as much as they consume |
| Average TTFT (Time to First Token) | 700ms | Current benchmark | Cloud platform latency dominates |
| Mobile network latency contribution | 35ms | 5% of total | Transport is no longer the bottleneck |
| Cloud platform latency contribution | 665ms | 95% of total | Compute proximity is the new constraint |

Sources: China Mobile Chairman Chen Zhongyue, 2026 Mobile Network Conference, November 6, 2026.

The last two rows are the most strategically significant. Chen broke down the end-to-end latency for an AI query: the mobile network contributes just 35 milliseconds — about 5% of the total 700ms round trip. The cloud platform, where the AI model actually processes the request, contributes 665ms, or 95%. The bottleneck has moved from transport to compute.

This inversion explains why China Mobile is building data centers instead of just upgrading cell towers. In the consumer internet era, the telecom's job was to move bits from the user to the cloud as fast as possible. In the AI era, the telecom's job is to ensure the intelligence itself is close enough to the user that the cloud round-trip doesn't destroy the experience. By building gigawatt-scale compute campuses distributed across China, China Mobile is effectively moving the cloud into the network.

## The Global Comparison: A Category of One

To understand how unusual China Mobile's strategy is, it helps to compare it with the two groups of companies it sits between: global telecom carriers and American hyperscalers. Neither comparison is flattering to the Western telecom industry.

| Carrier AI Compute Investment Comparison | 2026-2027 AI/Compute Capex | Primary AI Strategy | Foundation Model |
|---|---|---|---|
| China Mobile | ¥100B+ ($14-15B) compute-only in 2027 | Build gigawatt campuses, GPU clusters, full-stack AI | Jiutian (九天), 2T-parameter multimodal |
| China Telecom | Not yet disclosed for 2027 | Cloud-AI integration, government contracts | TeleChat (星辰) |
| China Unicom | Not yet disclosed for 2027 | Industry AI applications, enterprise services | Yuanjing (元景) |
| AT&T (US) | No dedicated AI compute capex line | Partner with Microsoft/OpenAI for enterprise AI | None — uses OpenAI via Azure |
| Verizon (US) | No dedicated AI compute capex line | AI for network optimization, customer service | None — uses Google/AWS AI |
| Deutsche Telekom (EU) | Limited, primarily partnerships | AI-enabled customer service, network ops | Partnership with Aleph Alpha |
| NTT (Japan) | Moderate, focused on enterprise AI | tsuzumi model, corporate AI services | tsuzumi (own model) |

Sources: Company filings, industry reports, November 2026.

The gap is stark. Neither AT&T nor Verizon has announced a dedicated AI compute investment at any scale. Both treat AI as a feature to be purchased from cloud providers — a tool for improving customer service chatbots and network operations, not a reason to build entirely new infrastructure. Deutsche Telekom has made partnership bets. NTT has developed its own lightweight model. But no Western carrier is building gigawatt-scale AI compute campuses.

The comparison with US hyperscalers reveals the opposite extreme. Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta each spend between $50 billion and $80 billion annually on data center infrastructure. But these are technology companies whose core business is computing. They do not operate nationwide wireless networks, maintain last-mile fiber infrastructure, or manage spectrum licenses. China Mobile is doing both — the telecom and the hyperscaler — simultaneously.

This hybrid position is unique in the global technology landscape. And it is a direct result of China's "compute-power network" (算力网络) national strategy, which treats computing infrastructure as a public utility on par with electricity and water — a strategic grid that the state must own and operate.

## The Sovereignty Dimension: State Compute as Strategic Asset

China Mobile's pivot cannot be understood outside the context of China's broader sovereign AI architecture. The company is not merely responding to market demand; it is executing a national directive that has been building since 2023.

The architecture has three layers. The first is chip supply — underwritten by the National Big Fund's investments in SMIC, Cambricon, and Huawei's Ascend program. The second is commercial AI labs — funded by provincial and municipal government guidance funds that have poured into companies like DeepSeek, Zhipu, and Moonshot AI. The third layer, where China Mobile operates, is state-owned-enterprise deployment.

This third layer is the largest and least visible. Conservative estimates place aggregate central-SOE AI capital expenditure for 2024-2026 above $100 billion — more than ten times the disclosed independent-lab funding tracked in venture databases. None of this appears as venture rounds or startup valuations. It flows through ordinary capex line items at companies like China Mobile, absorbed into budgets that look like infrastructure spending but are actually AI capacity building.

| China's Three-Layer Sovereign AI Stack | Role | Key Players | Annual Capital Flow |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chip supply layer | Domestic AI chip design and manufacturing | SMIC, Cambricon, Huawei Ascend, Enflame | Big Fund Phases 1-3: $50B+ committed |
| Commercial lab layer | Frontier model development and open-source ecosystem | DeepSeek, Alibaba Qwen, Zhipu, Moonshot AI, MiniMax | Provincial guidance funds, VC: $20-30B+ |
| SOE deployment layer | Compute infrastructure and production deployment | China Mobile (Jiutian), China Telecom (TeleChat), State Grid, Sinopec | $100B+ aggregate 2024-2026 (est.) |

Sources: Sovereign AI capital architecture analysis, company disclosures, 2026.

China Mobile's Jiutian (九天, "Nine Heavens") foundation model series is the product layer of this stack. The company began laying AI groundwork in 2013, established a dedicated Jiutian team in 2020, and has since released a two-trillion-parameter multimodal model, thirty industry-specific models, and a third-generation platform deeply integrated with its Computing-Network Brain orchestration system. Jiutian participated in the China Academy of Information and Communications Technology's "Token Service Capability Climbing Plan," which established national baselines for AI service quality — minimum 55 tokens per second output throughput, sub-0.9-second first-token latency, and 99.9% call success rates.

In January 2026, Jiutian capabilities were integrated into Zhejiang Mobile's 1-millisecond urban compute network — a deployment that puts AI inference within a millisecond of every user in one of China's most economically dynamic provinces. This is the model for what China Mobile plans to scale nationally. The company's Computing-Network Brain 3.0, released in 2025, functions as an orchestration layer that routes AI workloads across its geographically distributed infrastructure — matching each query to the compute resource that can handle it within the latency budget the application demands. It is, in effect, a national-scale inference scheduling system wearing a corporate logo.

## The Risks: Execution at Gigawatt Scale

For all its ambition, China Mobile's bet carries real risks that the company has not fully addressed publicly.

**Power supply.** Seven gigawatt-scale campuses require — by definition — seven gigawatts of reliable power capacity. China's eastern provinces, where demand is highest, face power constraints that have already forced data center projects to relocate to western regions like Inner Mongolia and Ningxia. The "east data, west computing" (东数西算) national project was designed to route compute demand to regions with surplus power, but it introduces latency penalties that conflict with China Mobile's low-latency AI ambitions. The company has not disclosed where its seven campuses will be located or how it will balance proximity against power availability.

**Chip sourcing.** China Mobile's GPU clusters depend on a supply chain it does not control. Export restrictions have cut off access to NVIDIA's most advanced accelerators. Domestic alternatives — Huawei's Ascend 950, Cambricon's MLU series, and newer entrants — are improving rapidly but still face manufacturing yield challenges at SMIC's DUV-limited fabs. The company's "intelligent computing capacity will double" commitment assumes a chip supply that is not yet guaranteed.

**Utilization economics.** Building compute is one thing. Selling it profitably is another. China's AI compute market is experiencing rapid price compression as supply floods in from multiple state-backed operators. Alibaba, Tencent, and ByteDance are all expanding their cloud AI capacity aggressively. The risk of overbuilding — of creating gigawatt-scale campuses that run at 40% utilization — is real and growing.

| China Mobile Compute Bet: Risk Assessment | Risk Factor | Severity | Mitigation Strategy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Power supply constraints | High | East-data-west-compute routing, on-site generation | Unclear |
| Advanced chip access | High | Domestic chip partnerships (Huawei, Cambricon) | In progress but yield-limited |
| Compute utilization rates | Medium | Guaranteed state-enterprise demand, 1B+ subscriber base | Strong but not proven |
| Technology disruption | Medium | Multi-architecture investment, open standards participation | Moderate |
| Regulatory/political shifts | Low | State-owned status provides alignment | Strong |

Sources: Industry analysis, company disclosures, November 2026.

## What Comes Next

China Mobile's November 6 announcement is a signal to every other player in China's AI ecosystem — and to the global telecommunications industry. The signal is this: in China, compute infrastructure is not a business decision. It is a national mission, executed by the companies best positioned to carry it out.

![The scale of China's compute buildout dwarfs what any Western telecom carrier has attempted](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1576091160399-112ba8d25d1d?w=800&q=80)

The timing also matters. China Mobile's 2027 compute spending plan lands in the same week that the company reported AI-generated content traffic growing five-fold year-on-year — data that transforms the investment from speculative to reactive. This is not a bet on future demand. It is a scramble to keep up with demand that has already arrived.

For China Telecom and China Unicom, the pressure to match China Mobile's commitment will be immediate. Their foundation models — TeleChat and Yuanjing — are less mature than Jiutian, and their compute infrastructure is smaller. But they will not be allowed to fall too far behind. Expect both to announce their own expanded AI investment plans before the end of 2026.

For the broader global industry, the implications are harder to ignore. The United States has no equivalent to China Mobile's compute buildout — no carrier that treats AI infrastructure as its core mission. The closest American parallel is the constellation of hyperscaler data center projects, but those are private companies serving private demand. China Mobile is a state instrument building public capability.

The question for 2027 will not be whether China Mobile can spend 100 billion yuan on compute. The question is whether anyone else in the world can match what that spending creates.
