The numbers flash red across every terminal on the trading floor. Hong Kong's Hang Seng Tech Index — home to Alibaba, Tencent, and the crown jewels of Chinese internet giants — has just touched a two-year low. The ChiNext Index, which hit a record high this past summer, is now down 31% for the year. The Star 50, China's chip-heavy answer to the Nasdaq, has watched its own record rally evaporate into thin air.

Combined, the three indexes have lost $1.7 trillion in market value since the end of June.

And here is the detail that stings the most: while every major Chinese tech benchmark now sits in negative territory for 2026, the United States, Taiwan, and South Korea are all still positive. The AI trade did not die. It simply left China.

How did the market that produced the "DeepSeek moment" — the shock heard round the world in January 2025, when a little-known Hangzhou lab proved Chinese AI could compete with Silicon Valley at a fraction of the cost — become the epicenter of the global AI selloff? The answer involves a toxic cocktail of excessive valuations, tightening US export controls, a growing compute gap, and a fundamental repricing of what China's AI companies can actually deliver when they cannot get the chips they need.

## What Actually Happened: The Anatomy of a Meltdown

The rout did not happen in a single day. It was a slow-motion collapse that began in late June and accelerated through November, punctuated by brief rallies that convinced investors the bottom had arrived. Each time, it had not.

The timeline tells the story:

| Date | Event | Market Impact |

| Nov 21-22, 2026 | Global AI rout accelerates | Chinese indexes hit fresh lows; $1.7T total loss confirmed |

The scale of the destruction is difficult to overstate. The Hang Seng Tech Index, which had surged on the back of Alibaba and Tencent's AI ambitions, is now trading at levels last seen in late 2024 — before the DeepSeek phenomenon, before the Qwen open-source wave, before the narrative of Chinese AI parity took hold.

The ChiNext Index's 31% year-to-date decline is even more remarkable considering it set a record high this summer. That means the index has fallen roughly 40% or more from its peak in just five months. The Star 50, which tracks China's semiconductor champions, has similarly wiped out what was once a record-breaking rally.

The asymmetry is the story. Every major Asian market experienced the November AI selloff. But only China's indexes have fully surrendered their 2026 gains — and then some.

## Why It Happened: Five Forces Colliding

### 1. The Valuation Hangover

Chinese AI stocks entered 2026 on a wave of euphoria. The "DeepSeek moment" of January 2025 had convinced global investors that Chinese AI was not just competitive but potentially superior on cost efficiency. Z.AI, the former Zhipu AI, saw its market value surge past HK$1 trillion after its Hong Kong listing. Optical module maker Zhongji Innolight reached all-time highs. Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corp (SMIC) traded at multiples that implied years of uninterrupted growth.

The problem was that these valuations were built on narratives, not earnings. When the narrative cracked, there was no floor.

"At current price levels, I still can't find value in these Chinese AI-related names," Jian Shi Cortesi of GAM Investment Management told Bloomberg. "Many stocks will need to fall another 30% to 50% to become very attractively valued."

That is a stunning statement. After a $1.7 trillion decline, a professional investor is saying the sector needs to lose another third to half of its remaining value before it becomes investable. It implies that the bubble was not merely large — it was structural.

### 2. The Chip Blockade Tightens

If valuations were the fuel, US export controls were the match. The most devastating development came in early November, when the US proposed rules that would restrict Chinese companies from accessing advanced Nvidia processors through overseas data centers.

This was a critical escalation. Chinese AI companies had been circumventing US chip bans by renting compute from data centers in Southeast Asia and the Middle East — a workaround that had become an open secret in the industry. The proposed rules would effectively shut that door.

The impact is already visible. Tencent warned that chip shortages could delay its next-generation HY4 model. Alibaba, which had committed at least 380 billion yuan ($53 billion) to AI infrastructure over three years, now expects cloud revenue growth to slow to between 30% and 45% next year before recovering above 50% in 2028 — a significant walk-back of earlier optimism.

The compute gap is showing up in model performance, too. Not a single entry among the 10 top-ranked models on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index now originates from China — a dramatic reversal from just four months ago, when Moonshot AI's Kimi K3 was competing head-to-head with OpenAI and Anthropic flagships.

| Model | Origin | Artificial Analysis Rank (Nov 2026) |

| Claude Opus 5.5 | US | Top tier |

This performance slide is not just a technical footnote. It is the fundamental driver of the investment thesis collapsing. If Chinese models cannot stay near the frontier, the entire narrative of Chinese AI parity — and the premium valuations that came with it — falls apart.

### 3. The Global AI Reckoning

China's rout is not happening in isolation. In November, the global AI trade experienced its most severe correction since the ChatGPT era began. The Nasdaq Composite slumped to a seven-month low. South Korea's KOSPI hit a nine-month low. Taiwan's benchmark fell 6.6% in a single day.

The trigger was growing anxiety about AI capital expenditure sustainability. With Meta reporting negative free cash flow for the first time since 2012, and the combined Big Three cloud providers carrying nearly $1.7 trillion in backlog against $600 billion in planned 2026 spending, investors began asking the question that had been lurking for months: when does the spending stop paying back?

But here is the critical distinction. In the US, the selloff is being driven by questions about return on investment — the technology works, but is it worth what people are paying? In China, the selloff is being driven by questions about capability itself — can Chinese companies even build the technology at the frontier if they cannot access the compute?

The first question is about profitability. The second is about survival.

### 4. The Regulatory Squeeze

Beijing's own regulatory actions have added another layer of uncertainty. In October, the Ministry of Commerce proposed adding 23 AI technologies to China's export-control catalogue, including large models and intelligent agents above certain performance thresholds. While open-source weights were excluded from the draft, the signal was unmistakable: Beijing views frontier AI as a strategic asset to be controlled, not just a commercial product to be sold.

This has created a paradox that investors are struggling to price. Domestically, China is building an increasingly elaborate regulatory framework — from anthropomorphic AI rules to agent governance to safety standards. Externally, it is contemplating export restrictions that could limit the overseas expansion of its most successful AI companies. Both moves make strategic sense from a national security perspective. Neither is good for valuations.

The contrast with the United States is instructive. While Washington debates how to restrict Chinese AI (DHS banning DeepSeek from federal agencies, states banning it on government devices), it has done little to slow the growth of its own AI champions. American AI companies face regulatory uncertainty too, but not the kind that directly threatens their core business model.

### 5. Weak Domestic Demand

Beneath the geopolitics and the technology race lies a more prosaic problem: Chinese consumers are not spending. Weak consumer spending has weighed on the entire tech sector, from e-commerce to advertising to cloud services. AI, for all its transformative potential, has not yet generated enough revenue in China to offset these macro headwinds.

ByteDance's Doubao leads the consumer AI market with 382 million monthly active users, and Alibaba's Qwen app has surged to 158 million. But consumer AI in China remains almost universally free. ByteDance only recently began exploring Doubao subscriptions, and the monetization path for AI assistants in a market accustomed to free services remains unproven.

Enterprise adoption is growing — Chinese AI startups raised over 110 billion yuan ($16.2 billion) in Q1 2026, up 185% year-on-year — but the revenue generated by AI services is still a rounding error compared to the capital being deployed to build them.

## The Anthropic Contrast

If you want to understand the depth of the divergence, look at what happened on November 19.

Anthropic, the American AI safety company, went public in what was described as a "bumper" IPO. The stock briefly touched a $2 trillion valuation. The listing was widely viewed as an important test of confidence in the AI trade — and it passed, at least initially, even amid the broader selloff.

The same week, Chinese AI indexes were hitting multi-year lows.

This is not just a story about one company's successful IPO. It is a story about where global capital believes the future of AI is being built. When investors want AI exposure, they are increasingly looking to American companies — even as those companies face their own valuation questions — because the compute restrictions have created a ceiling on what Chinese AI can achieve.

The numbers underscore the gap:

| Metric | US AI Leaders | Chinese AI Leaders |
|--------|--------------|-------------------|
| Top model on Artificial Analysis Index | Multiple in top 10 | Zero in top 10 |
| IPO market | Anthropic at $2T valuation briefly | Z.AI and MiniMax listed, but struggling |

| Cloud capex trajectory | $600B planned for 2026 | Alibaba slowing to 30-45% growth |
| Regulatory headwind | Antitrust, safety debates | Export controls + domestic restrictions |
| Consumer AI monetization | ChatGPT subscriptions, enterprise | Mostly free, limited subscription uptake |

## Where the Money Is Moving

The rout is not uniform. Within the wreckage, a new pattern is emerging — one that reflects the compute constraint at the heart of China's AI dilemma.

Domestic semiconductor manufacturers are the potential winners. With US restrictions closing off access to foreign chips, Chinese AI companies have no choice but to turn to domestic suppliers. SMIC, China's largest chip foundry, could see increased orders as companies like Huawei ramp up production of AI accelerators.

But this is a double-edged sword. SMIC trades at roughly 80 times forward earnings — a valuation that prices in years of flawless execution. Alibaba, by contrast, trades at roughly 10 times forward earnings. The market is paying a massive premium for the companies that make the chips and a massive discount for the companies that use them, because the chipmakers have pricing power in a supply-constrained environment while the AI labs are spending heavily on compute they can barely access.

This creates a strange dynamic where China's AI ambitions are being taxed at every level. The labs cannot get enough compute. The chipmakers who supply the compute are valued so highly that investing in them is itself a risky bet. And the entire sector is caught in a policy crossfire between Washington's restrictions and Beijing's own regulatory tightening.

## The Structural Problem Nobody Wants to Discuss

Beyond the immediate causes, the rout has exposed a deeper issue with China's AI investment thesis.

The original bull case was built on efficiency: Chinese AI companies could achieve near-frontier performance at dramatically lower cost. DeepSeek's R1 model demonstrated that a well-engineered system trained with limited compute could compete with models built by companies spending orders of magnitude more. This "efficiency advantage" was supposed to be China's answer to the chip blockade.

But the events of the past five months suggest that the efficiency advantage has limits. As US frontier models continue to push forward — with Anthropic's Mythos Preview, OpenAI's GPT-6.1 family, and Google's Gemini 4 series setting new benchmarks — Chinese models are struggling to keep pace. The open-source models that once seemed poised to capture the global market are now, in some cases, being released with deliberately reduced capabilities (as with the recent cyber-capability filtering by Alibaba, Tencent, and Moonshot) or without weights entirely (Qwen4-Max).

The efficiency narrative has been replaced by a ceiling narrative: Chinese AI can be good, but it cannot be the best, because the best requires compute that Washington will not allow Beijing to have.

This is a much harder story to sell to investors.

## What Comes Next: Three Scenarios

The path forward depends on forces that are largely outside the control of Chinese AI companies. Three scenarios are worth considering:

**Scenario 1: The Chip Wall Holds**

US restrictions tighten further, closing the overseas data center loophole entirely. Chinese AI labs are forced to rely on domestic chips (Huawei's Ascend series, SMIC's production) that remain 1-2 generations behind Nvidia's best. Chinese models fall further behind the frontier, and the valuation discount on Chinese AI stocks relative to US peers widens further. In this scenario, the rout has further to run.

**Scenario 2: Domestic Substitution Works**

Huawei and SMIC close the gap faster than expected. China's massive AI infrastructure spending — Alibaba's 380 billion yuan commitment, Tencent's 63% quarter-on-quarter capex increase — begins to show results. Chinese models stabilize near the frontier. Valuations bottom and gradually recover. This is the bull case, but it requires technological breakthroughs that have not yet materialized at scale.

**Scenario 3: The Global AI Trade Recovers**

The current selloff proves to be a correction, not a collapse. US AI spending continues, global risk appetite returns, and Chinese AI stocks recover as part of a broader tech rally. This scenario is possible but would not resolve the underlying compute constraint — it would simply mean investors are willing to look past it again.

Most analysts currently lean toward a version of Scenario 1, with elements of Scenario 3 providing intermittent relief rallies. The consensus view is that Chinese AI stocks will remain under pressure until either the geopolitical landscape shifts or domestic chip production reaches a level that removes the compute bottleneck.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Scale of destruction**: Chinese AI stocks across three major indexes have lost $1.7 trillion in market value since June 2026. All three indexes — Hang Seng Tech, ChiNext, and Star 50 — are now negative for 2026, while US, Taiwan, and Korean peers remain positive.

2. **Root cause is compute**: US chip export restrictions, including the proposed overseas data center rules, have created a hard ceiling on Chinese AI capabilities. No Chinese model currently ranks in the top 10 of the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index.

3. **Valuations remain stretched**: Even after the rout, analysts believe many Chinese AI stocks need to fall another 30-50% to reach attractive valuations. SMIC trades at ~80x forward earnings; Alibaba at ~10x.

4. **The Anthropic contrast**: While Chinese AI indexes hit multi-year lows, Anthropic's US IPO briefly reached a $2 trillion valuation. Global capital is making its preference clear.

5. **Domestic chips are the wild card**: SMIC and Huawei could benefit from forced domestic substitution, but the gap with Nvidia remains significant. The timeline for closing it is measured in years, not quarters.

6. **This is structural, not cyclical**: Unlike the US AI selloff, which is about ROI concerns on working technology, China's rout reflects doubts about capability itself. That makes it harder to recover from — and more dependent on geopolitical resolution than market dynamics.

---

*The $1.7 trillion question is not whether China can build good AI. It already has. The question is whether it can build the best AI when the best requires chips it cannot buy. Until that question is answered, Chinese AI stocks will trade at a discount that no earnings report can close.*
