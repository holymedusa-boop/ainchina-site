On the morning of November 24, in a conference room at the State Administration for Market Regulation in Beijing, four officials from three different government bodies sat before a packed press corps and made an announcement that received far less international attention than it deserved.

Sun Wei, deputy director-general of SAMR's standards innovation department, stated that China has spearheaded the development of **78 international standards on artificial intelligence** under the International Organization for Standardization and the International Electrotechnical Commission — the two bodies that effectively write the technical rulebook for the global technology industry. Of those, 29 are foundational standards covering data governance, human-machine interaction, and safety. Another 49 target sector-specific applications from smart cities to intelligent manufacturing. Nearly 400 Chinese experts are registered in ISO and IEC AI working groups. China holds the convenorship of two working groups within ISO/IEC JTC1/SC42, the primary international committee for AI standardization, and several more Chinese experts serve as project editors — the people who actually draft the text that becomes international law-by-technical-consensus.

Fan Kefeng, vice-president of the China Electronics Standardization Institute, then dropped the detail that should have made every compliance officer in Brussels sit up: **ISO/IEC 5259-4:2024**, an international standard on data quality process frameworks for analytics and machine learning led by Chinese experts, has been **adopted by European standardization bodies as a technical reference for AI compliance assessment**. A China-led standard is now informing how the European Union evaluates whether AI systems meet its regulatory requirements.

This is not a story about AI models, chips, or venture capital. It is a story about something more subtle and, in the long run, possibly more consequential: **the race to define the technical vocabulary of global AI governance**. And while the United States debates whether to call AI "super intelligence" in federal communications, and the European Union struggles to produce harmonized standards ahead of its own regulatory deadlines, China has been systematically and quietly filling the vacuum.

This is the comparative analysis of three governance models — and why China's standards-first approach may prove the most durable.

The SAMR press conference on November 24 was not a one-off event. It was the culmination of a coordinated effort across multiple government bodies — SAMR, the Ministry of Industry and Information Technology (MIIT), the Shanghai Municipal Market Regulation Bureau, and the China Electronics Standardization Institute — to present China's AI standardization progress as a unified national achievement.

The headline figures warrant careful parsing, because the number "78" actually refers to two distinct things, and understanding both reveals the scope of the strategy.

The "N" represents the sector-specific verticals — manufacturing, cities, water, logistics — where China has the world's deepest deployment experience and, therefore, the most practical knowledge to codify into standards.

## Three Models of AI Governance: A Comparative Framework

To understand why China's standards play matters, it is necessary to see it in the context of the two other major governance models competing for global influence. The United States, the European Union, and China have each adopted fundamentally different approaches to AI governance — and their relative effectiveness in 2026 reveals much about where the global governance center of gravity is shifting.

### The United States: Voluntary Frameworks and Federal Fragmentation

The American approach to AI governance in 2026 is characterized by its reliance on voluntary frameworks, sectoral regulators, and a growing patchwork of conflicting state laws. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0), published in January 2023, remains the cornerstone — a well-respected, flexible, and entirely voluntary framework organized around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, Manage. By early 2026, 68% of Fortune 500 companies reported implementing AI RMF core functions, and the framework is de facto mandatory for federal contractors.

But the US has conspicuously failed to produce binding federal AI legislation. In January 2025, the Trump administration revoked Executive Order 14110, the closest thing the US had to a unified AI governance policy. In its place, Executive Order 14434 of September 29, 2026 — titled "Inaugurating the Era of Super Intelligence" — directed federal agencies to use "Super Intelligence" and "SI" in place of "Artificial Intelligence" in public communications, a rebranding exercise that drew criticism for substituting terminology for substance.

The NIST AI Agent Standards Initiative, launched in February 2026 to develop standards for autonomous AI agents, has yet to publish a single standard as of November 2026 — only a request for information, a concept paper, listening sessions, and a blog post on identity management tenets. Meanwhile, individual states have filled the vacuum with their own laws: Texas HB 149 (effective January 2026), Colorado's AI Act, California's SB 53 on frontier AI — creating a regulatory patchwork that companies operating across state lines find increasingly difficult to navigate.

### The European Union: Binding Regulation, Lagging Standards

The European Union has chosen the opposite path: comprehensive, binding regulation through the AI Act, which entered into force in August 2024 and is being phased in through 2028. The AI Act is the world's most comprehensive AI law, establishing risk-based obligations that range from outright prohibitions on certain practices to stringent requirements for high-risk AI systems.

But the EU has encountered a critical implementation problem: **the standards needed to operationalize the regulation do not yet exist**. The AI Act grants a "presumption of conformity" to systems that comply with harmonized standards published in the Official Journal of the European Union — but as of late 2026, only one such standard had been published: **EN 18286:2026**, covering quality management systems for high-risk AI, which received its OJEU citation on October 14, 2026. The Digital Omnibus amendment of July 2026 postponed high-risk AI obligations from August 2026 to December 2027 precisely because the standards infrastructure could not keep pace with the regulatory timeline.

CEN-CENELEC JTC 21, the European committee responsible for developing AI Act harmonized standards, has a work programme encompassing approximately 35 technical standards. As of Q4 2026, most remain in development. The EU's regulatory ambition has outstripped its standardization capacity, creating a compliance vacuum that leaves both providers and enforcement authorities uncertain about how to demonstrate conformity.

### China: Standards as International Public Goods

China's approach is fundamentally different from both. Rather than imposing binding regulation first and developing standards later (the EU model), or relying on voluntary frameworks without federal legislation (the US model), China has pursued a **standards-first strategy** that treats international technical standards not as compliance overhead but as strategic assets — vehicles for exporting China's technological practices, reducing trade barriers for Chinese AI products, and shaping the global governance conversation on Beijing's terms.

The SAMR's framing is deliberate: standards are described as "global public goods" that "turn industrial practices and technological innovations into shared international resources." This is not merely rhetorical positioning. It reflects a calculated bet that in a world where every major economy is scrambling to regulate AI, the power to define the technical standards that underpin regulation is the power to shape regulation itself.

| Dimension | United States | European Union | China |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|-------|
| Primary instrument | Voluntary frameworks (NIST AI RMF) | Binding regulation (AI Act) | International standards (ISO/IEC) |
| Binding federal/national AI law | None | AI Act (2024/1689) | Generative AI Measures; sectoral rules |
| Standards development | NIST (domestic focus); no agent standard published as of Nov 2026 | CEN-CENELEC JTC 21 (~35 standards; 1 published) | 78 international standards led; 29 foundational + 49 application |
| AI agent governance | NIST Agent Standards Initiative (pre-standard) | Covered under AI Act high-risk framework | National standards on intelligent agents in translation pipeline |
| Enforcement mechanism | Sectoral regulators (FDA, SEC, FTC) | Market surveillance authorities; presumption of conformity | Market regulation via SAMR; certification and testing |
| International orientation | Domestic-first | Regional (EU/EEA) | Global (ISO/IEC leadership) |
| Key vulnerability | Federal fragmentation; state patchwork | Standards lag regulation; compliance uncertainty | Standards without domestic enforcement credibility |
| Notable 2026 milestone | EO 14434 (terminology change) | EN 18286:2026 first harmonised standard | ISO/IEC 5259-4 adopted by EU for compliance |

The comparative picture that emerges in November 2026 is striking. The US has world-class technical frameworks that lack legislative backing. The EU has comprehensive legislation that lacks the technical standards to make it operational. China has neither the US's research depth nor the EU's regulatory sweep — but it has something both lack: **a functioning, well-populated pipeline of international standards that other countries are already adopting**.

## The Landmark Standards: What China Actually Built

Beneath the headline numbers, three specific standards achievements stand out for their practical and symbolic significance.

### ISO/IEC 5259-4:2024 — The EU Adoption

The ISO/IEC 5259 series addresses data quality for analytics and machine learning — a foundational concern for any organization training or deploying AI systems. Part 4, led by Chinese experts, specifies lifecycle data quality management processes and technical requirements.

The EU's decision to adopt this standard as a technical reference for AI compliance assessment is the most concrete evidence that China's standards strategy is producing results. It means that when European companies and regulators evaluate whether an AI system meets data quality requirements — requirements that underpin the EU AI Act's own provisions — they are using a framework written under Chinese leadership.

Fan Kefeng's characterization was direct: "It means that this China-led standard will serve as an important technical reference for AI compliance assessments in the European Union. It underscored China's significant role in shaping international data governance rules."

### ISO/IEC TS 25568 — The Generative AI Governance First

The second landmark is perhaps the most consequential for the future of AI governance. Working with multiple countries, China has advanced **ISO/IEC TS 25568**, described as the world's first international standards proposal specifically targeting generative AI governance. The standard addresses risks that have become urgent global concerns: deepfakes, model poisoning, and the broader challenge of ensuring that generative AI systems behave reliably and safely.

This is not a domestic Chinese regulation being exported. It is a multilateral international standard, developed within ISO/IEC's consensus framework, that addresses generative AI risks with the same specificity that China's domestic Generative AI Measures (which have required filing for 748 services as of December 2025) bring to the domestic market. The difference is that TS 25568 carries international legitimacy — and Chinese fingerprints.

### Large Model Inference Security — The Technical Frontier

The third achievement addresses a frontier concern that most governance frameworks have yet to tackle. China has proposed an international standard on the security of large AI model inference services, building a comprehensive threat model that spans four domains: input, inference service, output, and infrastructure.

The threats it addresses are precisely those that keep AI security professionals awake at night: **prompt injection** (malicious inputs that override system instructions), **context poisoning** (corruption of the model's operating context), and **cross-tenant data leakage** (one user's data becoming visible to another in shared inference infrastructure). These are not hypothetical risks — they are active attack vectors against production AI systems worldwide.

That China is leading the international response to these threats, rather than reacting to standards written elsewhere, represents a significant shift in the global AI security conversation.

## The Translation Play: Standards as Export Infrastructure

Perhaps the most commercially savvy element of China's strategy is not the development of international standards but the systematic translation and export of domestic standards. SAMR has published 15 AI national standard translations covering large models and server system performance testing — in English and, significantly, in Khmer and Lao. A joint programme with MIIT will translate **78 national standards into 12 languages**, covering intelligent agents, smart terminals, and machine vision.

This is a trade strategy disguised as a technical initiative. When a Chinese AI company exports its products to Southeast Asia, Africa, or Latin America, the existence of Chinese-standard-aligned testing and certification frameworks in local languages reduces the cost and complexity of market entry. When a foreign company wants to sell AI products into markets where Chinese standards have been adopted as the local benchmark, it must comply with Chinese technical specifications.

| Language/Region | Standards Already Translated | Planned Translations | Strategic Target |
|----------------|------------------------------|---------------------|-----------------|
| English | 15 national AI standards | 78 national standards | Global markets |
| Khmer | Selected standards | Included in 12-language programme | Cambodia, ASEAN |
| Lao | Selected standards | Included in 12-language programme | Laos, ASEAN |
| Additional 9 languages | — | Part of 78-standard programme | Belt and Road markets |
| Free trade zone pilots | Shanghai, Hebei (5 zones) | Cross-border data flow testing | DEPA-aligned digital trade |

The free trade zone pilot programme adds another dimension. SAMR has launched first-batch pilots for "autonomous standardization opening-up" in five free trade zones, including Shanghai and Hebei, testing cross-border AI data flows and product conformity mutual recognition against rules such as the Digital Economy Partnership Agreement (DEPA). This is standards diplomacy at the trade-agreement level — using China's domestic regulatory sandbox infrastructure to test international standards alignment in controlled environments before scaling.

Cooperation channels have been established with ASEAN, EU, and Russian standardization bodies, including a **China-Russia working group on AI standardization** and conformity assessment for joint research on large-model capability evaluation methods. In a world where AI governance is increasingly intertwined with geopolitical alignment, these bilateral and regional channels give China options that neither the US nor the EU currently maintains at equivalent depth.

## The Geopolitical Chessboard

The timing of SAMR's announcement is not coincidental. It comes at a moment when the global AI governance landscape is in flux, and each of the three major powers faces distinct challenges that China's standards strategy is designed to exploit.

The United States, having withdrawn from multilateral governance initiatives under the current administration, finds itself without a coherent international AI governance presence. Its voluntary frameworks are respected but lack the binding force needed to shape international norms. Its executive order rebranding exercise has drawn criticism for substituting vocabulary for policy. And its AI agent standards initiative, launched with fanfare in February, has produced no actual standards ten months later.

The European Union, meanwhile, is discovering that writing regulation is easier than writing standards. The one-year gap between the AI Act's original high-risk compliance deadline and the actual availability of harmonized standards — a gap bridged only by the Digital Omnibus postponement — has damaged the EU's credibility as a governance model that others can follow. The EU's first-mover advantage in AI regulation is being eroded by its slow-moving standardization process.

China's wager is that it can fill this governance vacuum not with binding regulation (which would invite resistance) but with technical standards (which invite adoption). It is a wager that plays to China's institutional strengths: the ability to coordinate across government bodies and industry, the depth of its engineering talent pool, its manufacturing-scale deployment experience, and its willingness to invest in long-term institutional infrastructure rather than short-term regulatory headlines.

Shen Yi, director of the International Research Institute of Global Cyberspace Governance at Fudan University, articulated the logic: international standards "could turn governance principles into operational practices that are comparable and verifiable across markets. Such standards could help reduce duplicated testing and compliance costs for multinational companies, while lowering barriers for developing countries to participate in global AI governance."

That final phrase — "lowering barriers for developing countries" — is the key to China's pitch. The EU's AI Act is a compliance framework designed by and for wealthy, regulated markets. The US framework is a patchwork that makes sense only within American federalism. China's standards, translated into Khmer and Lao and tested in free trade zones, are designed for the markets where AI adoption is growing fastest and governance infrastructure is thinnest. If China can make its standards the default technical vocabulary for AI governance in the developing world, it will have achieved a form of soft power that neither chips nor tariffs can provide.

## What Could Go Wrong

The bullish case for China's standards strategy is compelling but incomplete. Several structural challenges could limit its effectiveness or even undermine its credibility.

### The Enforcement Credibility Gap

International standards derive their authority not from the consensus process that produces them but from the credibility of the institutions that enforce them. China's domestic AI governance record — content censorship requirements embedded in the Generative AI Measures, mandatory filing systems that give the state visibility into AI deployments, and the absence of independent judicial review — creates a fundamental tension with the neutral, technical framing of its international standards push. A standard on AI safety written under Chinese leadership and enforced within China's domestic political system carries different implications than the same standard written and enforced in a jurisdiction with independent oversight.

This gap matters most in developed markets, where regulators and civil society organizations are increasingly attentive to the governance implications of technical standards adoption. The EU's adoption of ISO/IEC 5259-4 may be a pragmatic recognition of technical quality, but it also invites scrutiny of the standard's provenance and the process that produced it.

### The Proposal-to-Standard Pipeline

Not all of China's 78 standards are finished products. Several items announced by SAMR remain proposals or pre-studies rather than published international standards. ISO/IEC TS 25568, the generative AI governance standard, is described as a "proposal" — a status that precedes the full consensus process, including technical scrutiny, national body review, and potential amendment. The distance between a well-crafted proposal and a widely adopted international standard can be years, and the process can expose technical weaknesses or political resistance that slow or derail adoption.

### The Multilateral Constraint

ISO and IEC operate on consensus principles that give every participating nation a voice. China's growing influence in these bodies is real, but it is not unlimited. Other nations — particularly the United States, Japan, South Korea, and EU member states — retain significant voting power and technical expertise within JTC1/SC42 and related committees. If China's standards push is perceived as too overtly geopolitical, it could trigger a counter-mobilization within the standards bodies themselves, leading to contested votes, parallel standards, or the fragmentation of the international standards ecosystem into competing blocs.

## The Verdict

China's 78 international AI standards represent something that neither the US's voluntary frameworks nor the EU's binding regulation has achieved: **a functioning, populated, and increasingly adopted international standards infrastructure for AI governance**. The EU's adoption of ISO/IEC 5259-4 as a compliance reference is not an anomaly — it is a proof of concept.

The comparative analysis reveals a governance paradox. The United States has the deepest AI research ecosystem and the most innovative technology companies, but its governance approach is fragmented across federal inaction and state-level patchwork. The European Union has the most comprehensive AI legislation, but its standardization process cannot keep pace with its regulatory ambition. China has neither America's research depth nor Europe's legal sophistication, but it has something both lack: **institutional patience and a willingness to do the unglamorous technical work of writing standards, building consensus, and translating documents into Khmer and Lao**.

This is not to suggest that China's approach is unproblematic. The enforcement credibility gap is real, the proposal-to-standard pipeline is uncertain, and the multilateral constraint means that China's influence within ISO and IEC is real but not unlimited. The standards China writes reflect China's governance philosophy — one that prioritizes state visibility, content control, and managed innovation over the open, pluralistic approach that Western democracies favor. Every standard adopted is a small victory for that philosophical framework.

But the verdict that matters is not whether China's standards approach is ideologically preferable — it is whether it is **effective**. And on that measure, the November 24 announcement provides compelling evidence that China's standards-first strategy is producing results that the US and EU have not matched. The EU is adopting China-led standards for its own compliance assessments. Developing markets are receiving Chinese standards in their own languages. International working groups are convening under Chinese leadership.

The rules of global AI governance are being written. The surprise is not that China is participating in that process. The surprise is how far ahead it has gotten while the rest of the world was watching the models, the chips, and the money — and not the standards documents where the real long-term power lies.

In a conference room in Beijing on a November morning, four officials made that point as clearly as it has ever been made. The question now is whether anyone outside China was paying attention — and if not, how many more standards will be written before they start.
